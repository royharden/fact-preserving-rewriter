The household had grown accustomed to the traveller's habit of considering every invitation before replying. This was regarded by the impatient as hesitation and by the charitable as discretion. The traveller offered no correction to either party. It was possible to enjoy a quiet afternoon without first settling the meaning of every preference, and there were occasions on which an unanswered question made better company than an argument. The drawing room, having survived many arguments, seemed particularly well suited to such an afternoon.

Near the window stood a table whose purpose was revised whenever a guest arrived. It had supported flowers, correspondence, and an ambitious arrangement of refreshments, though never with equal success. Its owner maintained that versatility was an advantage in furniture. Her brother suspected that a permanent arrangement would have spared them considerable trouble. Neither opinion prevented the table from being moved again, for a visitor had expressed a fondness for the window, and hospitality was allowed to interrupt even the most settled domestic theory.

Conversation turned toward the proposed excursion, which appeared agreeable until the practical details were mentioned. There was the question of weather, the question of companions, and the question of whether agreeing too readily would create expectations of future enthusiasm. The youngest guest believed enthusiasm improved by being exercised. The eldest preferred to reserve it for occasions that had already proved successful. Between these positions the plan advanced slowly, acquiring qualifications with such grace that its original simplicity was scarcely missed.

The traveller listened with interest and found that each objection concealed a preference which might have been stated more plainly. Yet plainness has its social costs. To say that a road is inconvenient may be kinder than admitting that its company is unwelcome; to praise a quiet evening may avoid explaining why a party inspires fatigue. These translations required no deliberate dishonesty. They were the household's familiar means of preserving ease, and ease was valued by people who did not always agree about what would produce it.

At length a servant brought the misplaced letter, and the excursion received attention of a different kind. The invitation had left room for choice where the company had imagined an obligation. This discovery was greeted with the modest relief appropriate to a difficulty of their own construction. Nobody proposed a formal apology to the afternoon. The traveller, whose patience had begun to seem prophetic, folded the letter and suggested that tea might be enjoyed before any further arrangements were attempted. The suggestion met with immediate approval.

During tea, a disagreement about the garden developed into a comparison of childhood recollections. The garden itself was visible beyond the glass and offered little assistance. A path remembered as broad proved narrow, while a tree described as ancient had been planted within living memory. Such corrections did not diminish the pleasure of remembering. They merely demonstrated that affection had been an active editor. The traveller thought there might be kindness in allowing an agreeable recollection to keep its proportions, provided nobody proposed to measure the present garden by them.

When the visitor rose to leave, every possible account of the afternoon had become available. It might be called productive because a plan was clarified, peaceful because no disagreement lasted, or lively because conversation had rarely paused. The household selected the account most suitable to its own disposition. The traveller was content to remember the open window and the ease with which a difficult proposal had become an ordinary cup of tea. There are social successes too small for an announcement and too useful to be dismissed merely because they arrive without ceremony.
