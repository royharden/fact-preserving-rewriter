Start with the symptom. State what stopped working. Keep the description small enough to compare with a trace. Then name the next action. A reader should not have to decode a mood before finding the work. If the evidence is thin, say so. Confidence belongs beside the observation that earned it. It does not belong in an opening flourish. Put assumptions where someone can challenge them. A quiet assumption can survive several reviews and still break the next change.

Use a short heading when the subject changes. Keep the heading concrete. The paragraph below it should carry the detail that the heading promises. Do not hide a decision in a sentence about background. Bring the decision forward, explain the reason, and attach the constraint. If the constraint expires, make that visible. An old workaround is easy to copy when its expiry condition has disappeared from the note. State the condition in ordinary language. Someone tired will read it later.

Separate observation from explanation. A connection dropped. That is an observation. A busy worker caused the drop. That is an explanation, and it needs evidence. The distinction saves time when the first explanation fails. You can keep the observation and replace the theory. If the note ties them together too tightly, the next reader has to unwind the entire story. Keep the useful pieces independent. The next investigation may start from a different symptom and arrive at the same trace.

Prefer verbs that point to a visible action. Open the trace. Compare the records. Pause the rollout. Leave the queue intact. A vague request creates another round of interpretation before anyone can act. Precision does not require formal language. It requires choosing the object and the action. Add the reason when it changes how the action should be performed. Skip a reason that merely repeats the instruction. Readers can tell when a sentence is making work look larger than it is.

Keep the handover useful. Say what is ready, what is waiting, and what would unblock it. Link the unresolved question to its owner. Do not make the next person reconstruct your afternoon. A good handover can be read while another task is already demanding attention. It should survive that interruption. Give the reader a place to resume. Put a risky condition near the action it constrains, rather than collecting every warning in a distant paragraph that will be skimmed.

When a check fails, keep the failure visible. Do not tidy the record until it resembles success. The failed attempt may explain why a later path is safer. Keep enough context to reproduce the difference. Avoid an inventory of unrelated changes. A small record with a clear comparison is more useful than a long record with no boundary. If you cannot reproduce the failure, record that limit. The next person can then judge whether another investigation is warranted.

Review the final state before writing the closing note. Remove stale claims. Replace intention with what actually changed. A planned check is still planned until there is a result. A result belongs to the version that produced it. Keep that attachment intact. Otherwise a minor edit can inherit a confidence it never earned. This is ordinary maintenance, like closing a valve after a trial. It takes less effort than finding out later that the record described a different state.

Leave the workspace legible. A temporary file should have an obvious purpose. A saved trace should say what it captured. A removed workaround should take its obsolete instructions with it. Cleanup is part of the change because the next task will begin in the room you leave behind. Finish the bounded job. Describe its remaining limits. Let the reader decide what to do next with evidence that is easy to inspect.
